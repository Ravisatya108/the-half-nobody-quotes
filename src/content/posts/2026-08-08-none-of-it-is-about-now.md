The dashboard is green.

Take a company at the size where this bites hardest. Somewhere past ten million in revenue, a couple of hundred people, a founder who used to sit in on the deals that mattered. On the slide: recurring revenue well up on last year, net retention over par, pipeline full.

Those are not one company's numbers. They are a composite of a shape I have watched many times, and the shape is the only part that matters. Revenue climbing. Churn flat. Satisfaction holding. A short board meeting.

By the time it is obvious that company has stalled, nobody can point to the quarter it turned.

You know the received advice, because it is the one sentence everybody in an operating role can recite. Be data-driven. You can't manage what you can't measure. Instrument everything, put the number on the wall, and let it settle the argument instead of the loudest person in the room.

I want to grant it full weight, because it earned its authority honestly. Before the dashboard, budget went where the founder's instinct pointed, and the instinct was usually a story about the founder.

Measurement broke that. It made expensive nonsense visible, and it gave quiet people a way to win. Anyone who has watched a team kill a beloved feature on the evidence has seen it work as advertised.

So for a long time I assumed the failure here was *bad* metrics. Vanity numbers. Measuring the easy thing instead of the true one. The fix, in that reading, is better instrumentation.

Then I sat down to write the better number, and kept the list of what I tried.

A weighted health score first: product usage, ticket volume, whether the executive sponsor still turns up to the quarterly. Every input there is a logged event, and logging comes after somebody has decided to stop caring. The score does not catch the decision. It catches the residue.

Product usage on its own, then. But usage is reported across the accounts that renewed. That is the survivors' number, and the survivors are the accounts I was never going to need warning about.

A leading-indicator dashboard, finally. Whatever goes in it has to be a field somebody fills in. The signals that arrive first are not fields — which is why they were never on the dashboard.

Three candidates, all broken in the same place, and the place was not the definition. It was the clock.

**These numbers are not wrong. Not one of them is about now — and each fails differently, for a structural reason you can check against your own records this afternoon.**

## Three rows, three reasons

Start with revenue. It is green, and it should be. Revenue this quarter is the settlement of decisions taken a long time ago. A deal closing in March entered the pipeline in the autumn, and the reputation that got someone to take the first call is older than the pipeline. The number is not describing how the company works now. It is paying out on how it worked then.

Check it in your own system. Open the last ten deals you closed, look up the first-touch date on each, take the median distance to close. That is your revenue lag, measured rather than asserted.

And it widens when things go well. The faster you grow, the larger the share of this quarter's number that was originated by a smaller, earlier version of the company. Acceleration pushes the number further into the past.

Churn is flat, and it would be. Churn does not measure dissatisfaction; it measures terminated contracts, a different event with a long gap in front of it. A customer whose service degrades in month two of an annual agreement cannot say so until renewal — later still if procurement is slow.

Disappointment is continuous. Cancellation is discrete, and you scheduled it.

Same move on the accounts you lost this year. For each, find the month the relationship actually degraded: the first missed response window, the handoff to someone who didn't know their history, the ticket that sat nine days. Then measure to the cancellation date. That gap is not slow reporting. It is written into the contract.

The third row fails differently, and it is not late at all. A satisfaction survey is voluntary, and voluntary response is not random. People answer while they are still engaged, and engagement tracks satisfaction. The sample is drawn from the customers you were never worried about.

The account on its third owner in a year, quietly pricing your competitors, is not answering your survey. They have stopped talking to you. That silence is the entire signal, and the instrument is built to discard it.

Which gives the row its ugly property: as service degrades, the unhappy leave the sample first, and the score can *rise* while the reality falls.

Different pull for this one. Get the response rate out alongside the score. If the score held while the response rate fell, you cannot read that as improvement — the sample changed underneath it. Find out who stopped answering before you decide what the number means.

Now the three together.

Three unrelated mechanisms: a settlement delay, a contractual window, a self-selecting sample. Two report the past. The third reports a room the unhappy have already walked out of. Not one reports the company you have today.

And because they fail in unrelated ways, they never contradict each other. They agree. Agreement across independent instruments is what all of us are trained to read as confirmation.

## What arrives on time

The early-warning signals do exist. They simply have no unit, so they never enter the record.

The conversation that stops when you come round the corner. The manager whose objections stopped arriving. The engineering team that has quietly given up on ambition — a run of planning sessions where every proposal is safe, from people who were pitching risky ones not long ago.

Your best people updating profiles they hadn't touched in years. The offer acceptance rate drifting down while everyone agrees it is market conditions. And your own three in the morning.

Every one of those is current. They are happening today, in the company that exists today.

How far behind does that put the dashboard? Take the two lags you just measured. They do not add — the revenue lag runs on deals you haven't closed yet, the churn lag on accounts you already have. Different populations.

So there is no single figure here, and I will not invent one. There is your revenue lag, there is your churn lag, and the only question worth asking is whether either is shorter than the interval at which the board meets.

Which is why a better dashboard does not fix it. Be data-driven is not a measurement policy. It is a rule of evidence — it decides what may count as a reason inside a room. Try telling a board the hallways are wrong. You will be asked, reasonably and immediately, what the data says.

The data says green.

So the discipline adopted to stop people winning arguments on vibes is the same discipline that makes the only current testimony inadmissible. The founder is not blind. The founder is holding evidence the room will not accept.

And the unease you have been talking yourself out of since the second row is not a mood. It is an observation with a sample size of one, arriving on time, from the only instrument in the building pointed at the present.
