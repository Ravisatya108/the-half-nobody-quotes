Every design review arrives at the same sentence. People can only hold about seven things in their head — seven, plus or minus two — so cut the nav, cut the bullets, cut the tiers. Show less. It is said with the confidence of a settled finding, because there is a famous paper behind it.

Before any argument about the paper, five seconds. You are the equipment here, so the result is yours.

Here is a phone number. Read it once, look away from the screen, and say it back.

3847290583

Now this one. Same instruction — read it once, look away, say it back.

384-729-0583

Same ten digits. Same order. Same you, five seconds apart. Nothing was taken away. Two hyphens were added, and the second string holds where the first one slides.

You have seen those digits twice, which helped the second string; the clean run is somebody else's — hand both to a friend in reverse order and watch which survives.

That is a problem for the sentence everyone repeats. If your limit were a limit on how much you can be shown, the second string should have failed as hard as the first — it contains exactly as much. Whatever the limit counts, it is not digits.

The standard reading deserves its due; it works constantly. Cut a navigation bar down and people find things faster, and every designer who has watched comprehension rise after a cut is holding honest evidence.

It works for a reason the sentence never states: to someone who does not know your material, every item on the screen genuinely is one item. Nothing has been grouped for them yet, so subtracting is your only lever.

But it is the lever of last resort, and the paper hands you the other one.

George Miller published the finding in *Psychological Review* in 1956, under a title people quote half of: *The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two*. The other half is *Some Limits on our Capacity for Processing Information*, and "some" is his hedge, not mine.

The chunk arrives in the last quarter, after four sections about other things, and Miller introduces it while admitting he cannot define it sharply — "familiar units or chunks, and a great deal of learning has gone into the formation of these familiar units."

What he draws from it: "Since the memory span is a fixed number of chunks, we can increase the number of bits of information that it contains simply by building larger and larger chunks, each chunk containing more information than before."

That is not a ceiling on content. It is an instruction to compress. **The slot count is not yours to raise — Miller puts it near nine for binary digits and near five for words. What one slot carries is the part you build.**

He would not even bless the seven. His last sentence about it: "But I suspect that it is only a pernicious, Pythagorean coincidence." The number was never the finding. The unit was.

## The board that could have gone the other way

In 1973 William Chase and Herbert Simon published "Perception in Chess" in *Cognitive Psychology*, and ran the version of this that can fail.

Three players sat for it: a master, a Class A player, and a beginner. Five real mid-game positions, five seconds each, then the board goes away and you rebuild it. First-trial averages: about sixteen pieces, eight, and four. That proves nothing on its own; masters might simply have better memories.

So they ran a second condition, and this is what turns a demonstration into a test. The same positions, the same pieces, replaced at random on the board. Five seconds again.

The result, in their words: "In the random, unstructured positions there was no relation at all between memory of the position and playing strength."

The advantage did not shrink. It disappeared, and then went further: first-trial recall on the scrambled boards was "even poorer than B's performance on the actual game positions" — B being the beginner, who managed four. Strip the structure and the master falls below what the novice held on a real board.

That is the entire argument, and it stands on one move: if the master's edge came from capacity, scrambling the pieces would have changed nothing. It changed everything. So the edge was never capacity.

I should say where my own version broke, because it broke twice. I have repeated the popular telling more than once: the master sees the board for five seconds and puts all twenty-five pieces back, every one right.

Then I opened the paper. The master "averaged only 16 pieces." The twenty-three-or-twenty-four-out-of-twenty-five figure is real, but it belongs to Adriaan de Groot's grandmasters and masters, in an earlier study on quieter positions — a gap Chase and Simon flag and explain on the page.

Losing that number cost me nothing. The second break was the expensive one: I had also been carrying five or six pieces each, statistically identical, as the scrambled-board result — and that figure is not in the paper.

The first number was decoration. The second was the argument, and the correction runs against the story I liked. That is usually how you tell which is which.

## Every hyphen you have ever typed was an instruction

Nothing about your equipment changed between those two strings. The hyphens did the work by telling you where to cut.

Every formatting decision you make is that hyphen. A header says: the previous group is finished, release it, start a new one. Bold says: this is the handle, and what surrounds it hangs off it. White space is a boundary drawn in nothing.

Show less and chunk better come from the same diagnosis and produce opposite artifacts. Show less deletes the seventh item and calls the problem solved. **Chunk better keeps all ten and groups them into three** — and the reader carries more away than the cut version would have given.

There is a cost buried in this, and it lands hardest on the people most likely to be reading this. Neither paper tests what follows; it is what the mechanism implies.

Chunks are learned, separately in each domain, over hundreds or thousands of exposures. You cannot lend yours out. The phrase that costs you one slot — the one that arrives whole, implications attached — costs the person you are designing for a word at a time.

It is the precise reason your work overloads the people it was built for, and from the inside it feels like clarity.

The test for that is as cheap as the one you already ran. Hand the thing to somebody who does not have your library, and watch where they stop.

You cannot give a reader another slot. You can only decide what one of theirs is worth.

*This came out of a habit of going back to the paper behind the sentence — not the summary, and not the version I had been repeating.*
