This week I read a question from a dentist. He owns a small practice, places his own implants, and opens with a line most people would spend a paragraph avoiding.

"Straight up, I am a bad businessman. I'm only where I am because of competent past management keeping me from screwing up too badly."

Every Christmas since he took over the practice he has run a deal — usually crowns, usually bridges, a way of getting loyal patients into treatment they'd been putting off. This year he tried implants. He took his usual price and cut it in half: eight hundred dollars, for the month of December, with him making the surgical guide himself.

He normally places ten to fifteen implants a month. In December he placed fifty-six.

The rest of his numbers are unusually honest. His practice costs $200–220 an hour to break even. His cost per implant ran about $180. His overhead for the month came in at 37% — and he immediately takes that back, because it was a lab-heavy month and the bill hadn't landed yet, so "in reality it is probably going to be around 42-43%."

Then the question: "As far as I can tell this worked out great and I'm not seeing why this shouldn't be my regular starting point price for uncomplicated straight forward implant cases. What am I missing here from a business perspective?"

The industry has a ready answer, and it arrived on schedule. Never compete on price. Discounting devalues the work. The most-upvoted reply says it cleanly: "Cheap implants can bump volume but strain staff, increase follow up visits, and devalue the procedure in patients' eyes. Keep promos short term so it feels special, not standard pricing."

Thirty-eight replies. They are all good-faith and most are experienced. One reframes it as the razor-and-blades model. One warns that harder cases will start showing up. One notes that a fifty-percent cut costs him roughly seventy-five percent of his profit, which is arithmetically sharp and entirely correct.

And they are all arguing about the same thing: whether a low number damages a seller.

Not one of the thirty-eight asks who the fifty-six were.

He told them. It's sitting mid-sentence, inside the line where he reports the volume, in the place details go to be skimmed: "Went from averaging 10-15 implants in an average month and placed 56 including around 20 that would be your typical limited exam and pop out a tooth type visits."

A limited exam and a tooth coming out is not a person who was weighing an implant. It's someone who walked in that morning with a problem and left without a tooth.

Here is the half nobody quotes: a discount devalues your work by moving buyers you already had *down a ladder*. That's the whole mechanism. Someone who would have paid full price pays less, learns the number was soft, and waits for the next sale.

Roughly twenty of the fifty-six were not on the ladder.

So run the sort on his own numbers. Fifty-six placements. Take out the twenty who arrived through an emergency visit and thirty-six remain, against a normal month of ten to fifteen — so the planned side of his practice roughly doubled or tripled too, and some of that is genuinely people moving down a ladder. The received wisdom has a real claim on that half.

It has no claim at all on the other twenty. Those people were not in his treatment plans, were not weighing his usual price, and did not choose eight hundred dollars over sixteen hundred. Whatever they chose it over, it wasn't an implant.

Which means it was the gap. The alternative to replacing a tooth on the day it comes out is not a cheaper implant somewhere else. It's an empty socket that closes over while you decide, and a decision that quietly makes itself.

That competitor had been beating him eleven months a year. His practice was already doing extractions every month — that's routine work in any general practice — and placing ten to fifteen implants against them. The extractions were always happening. The replacements mostly weren't. Nobody in that thread named the thing he was actually losing to, because it doesn't have a price list.

And notice what the eight hundred dollars did for that person, because it isn't what a discount does. It didn't make expensive work look cheap. It replaced an unknown number with a known one, spoken out loud, at the precise moment a human being is least equipped to absorb an unknown number — unplanned, unbudgeted, in the chair, right after bad news.

That's not the seductive half of a buying decision. That's the frightening half, and it got quieter.

Now the honest complications, because his December has more than one candidate explanation and I won't pretend otherwise.

December is when annual dental benefits expire, which pushes planned treatment into the last six weeks of the year everywhere, at every price. He has also run a Christmas deal every year he's owned the place — "It's always been a good event" — so his patients are trained to wait for one.

And in the same sentence where he sets the price, he mentions the other thing he changed: he started making the guides himself and training his assistants on 3D printing.

That last one matters more than it looks. He didn't only change what the work cost. He changed how fast it could happen, and by whom, in his own building. Two variables moved in December, and the plan on the table credits one.

All three of those confounds have something in common. Expiring benefits, a trained annual-deal expectation, a faster in-house workflow — every one of them acts on the patient who was already planning something.

None of them explains a person who walked in because a tooth broke.

That's why the twenty carry the case. They're the one group the alternative explanations can't reach.

He can settle this in an afternoon, and he is better placed to than almost any seller alive. Fifty-six people bought from him inside the last two months. He has their names, their phone numbers and their appointment times, in software he already owns. Most businesses would pay real money for a list like that and never assemble one.

He asked a forum instead.

One question does it, asked of the extraction patients rather than the planned ones: *at the moment you heard the number, what did you think was going to happen instead?*

If they say they'd have done it in the spring anyway, the thread was right, he cannibalised his own book, and he should put the price back. If they say they figured they'd just live with the gap, then he didn't discount anything. He found a product he had never put on sale, and priced it for the first time.

Half of sixteen hundred is a discount.

The price of a thing nobody was buying is just a price.
