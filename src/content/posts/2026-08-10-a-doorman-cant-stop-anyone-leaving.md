Last October an agency owner posted his whole month. Not a summary of it — the actual ledger, every account gained and every account lost, itemised down to the dollar. Almost nobody does this.

He'd been at it six years. "I've always done month to month, cancel anytime. Been able to get to roughly 70k-ish MRR but have stalled." His diagnosis of himself was one line: "Very much has become a ferris wheel of onboarding clients and off boarding clients."

He sells two things. Ads or search work at $650 a month, both together at $1,000.

His fix, announced in the same post: a three-month minimum, higher prices, and an explicit exit from the bottom of his market. "Unfortunately, this does mean that we won't be able to be as helpful to smaller budget clients. But we've outgrown that category and need to continue to take steps forward, imo."

Then the question he actually wanted answered, typed the way people type when they mean it: "I think im at the point where i'd rather work with less customers for longer than work with more customers for lesser (is that right). But who knows maybe this is what keeps this thing growing"

The industry has a ready answer and it arrived from someone with twenty years in: "Raise your prices to weed out clients that are looking for quick results versus long term, strategic partnerships. This doesn't have to be a significant price hike, but even raising your lowest package to $2k/month will help on this front."

He took it whole. "W response! that all makes way too much sense - i'm not doing any of those things sufficiently enough lol i'll work on adding some of these into the mix immediately - ty for your response"

I want to test that advice against the ledger sitting directly above it — because he published two long strings of numbers, and he published how they were totalled: "(Btw - I use chat gpt prompt and spreadsheet for it to do the math for me)".

So I added them up.

The twenty-five accounts he gained come to $12,400. He reported $12,950. The seventeen he lost come to $12,550. He reported $12,100. The month he describes as "up overall $850" is actually down a hundred and fifty dollars.

Sixty-nine replies. Nobody added them up.

That's the smaller of the two things in those strings, though. The larger one is what happens when you stop totalling them and start sorting them.

His average lost account was $738 a month. His average gained account was $496.

Five of the seventeen departures were $1,000 or more. One was $2,000 — twice the price of the most expensive thing he sells. Against that, only two of his twenty-five new accounts reached $1,000, and sixteen of them came in below his entry package.

Seventy-six percent of the revenue that walked out that month walked out at or above his $650 price.

Here is the half nobody quotes: raise your prices to weed out the churners, and you've assumed the churners are at the bottom. This ledger says the opposite. He wasn't losing cheap, impatient clients and keeping his good ones. He was losing his biggest accounts and replacing them with smaller ones.

The fix he announced — raise the floor, stop serving small budgets — was aimed at the quarter of his bleeding that wasn't the problem.

And there's a mechanical reason it was never going to work, which has nothing to do with whether $650 is too cheap. It probably is too cheap. That's a separate and real problem.

A price is a doorman. It stands at the entrance deciding who gets in. It is a genuinely powerful instrument and it has exactly one jurisdiction: **admission**. It has no authority whatsoever over the exit.

Every one of those seventeen accounts was already inside the building.

Look at what he does have on the way out. Month to month. Cancel anytime. No annual option. No notice period. No termination terms. Nothing that happens when a client clicks cancel except the cancellation. His clients make a fresh decision to stay twelve times a year and it costs them nothing to make it differently.

The three-month minimum is the right kind of instrument, and it's set to the wrong number by his own reasoning. He says it "gives us a much better time frame (90 days) to work through our processes" — which means ninety days is roughly when search work starts producing something worth judging.

So the term ends at exactly the moment the client first has evidence, and the first real renewal decision gets made on the thinnest case he'll ever have. A commitment should end after the proof, not on top of it.

Worth noting: the highest-scoring reply in that thread wasn't the price advice. It was someone recommending notice periods and termination fees — the one suggestion aimed at the exit rather than the entrance. He didn't act on it.

Ten months later, the rest of this is written in his own hand.

In January he reported that the change had worked. "January started at around $60k MRR. We're ending the month around $70–72k MRR, despite actively raising prices and letting some clients go." He'd moved his legacy accounts up and set a new floor at $1,000. His verdict: "Raising prices didn't slow demand — it filtered it."

Read those two numbers again against the October post. He'd been at "roughly 70k-ish" when he started. Seventy to seventy-two thousand isn't a recovery from sixty. It's the number he began with, measured from the dip in between.

Then, this summer: "i was looking at our revenue and we are actually down about 10k MRR between june and july. we had a 5k client that had to cancel, as well as a few others the pauses/cancelled."

A five-thousand-dollar client. In October the largest account he lost was two thousand, and that was already double his top package.

Do the arithmetic the price rise performs on the shape of a business. To hold $70,000 a month at an average account of about $500, you need around a hundred and forty clients. At an average of $1,400, you need about fifty. Same revenue, a third of the accounts — so every account is suddenly worth three times as much of the whole.

A fourteen percent churn rate across a hundred and forty clients is weather. You staff for it. The same rate across fifty clients is a series of events, and each one leaves a hole you can see from across the room.

His worst single loss went from $2,000 to $5,000 while he was doing everything right.

I'm not claiming the price rise caused that cancellation; one client leaving has its own story, and he names a cause himself — he took June and half of July off, and his lead flow is visibly tied to him. That's honest and it's probably true. A $5,000 client is also proof the move upmarket partly worked. He genuinely does have a better business than he had.

The narrower thing is this: the same strategy that produced a five-thousand-dollar client is what made losing one cost five thousand dollars. Raising prices doesn't slow the leak. It makes each drop bigger.

Which brings it back to the question he asked and nobody answered.

Fewer customers for longer is a good trade. It might be the best trade in professional services. But it has two halves, and he only ever tested one.

"Fewer" is a decision. He made it, executed it, and it worked.

"Longer" is a measurement — and across three posts, ten months and one fully itemised ledger, there is not a single number anywhere for how long a client stays.

Fewer is something you can decide on a Tuesday. Longer is a number, and he still doesn't have it.

*Behind this piece: a pricing-diagnosis discipline that separates what a price does at the door from what holds a customer once they're inside — plus his own ledger, added up.*
