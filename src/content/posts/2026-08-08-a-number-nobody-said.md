The chairs are in a rough circle at the end of a workshop day and somebody has just said his day rate out loud. It sits there the way a number sits in a room of people who all sell the same thing. Then, from across the circle, kindly, the way you hand somebody a coat: *charge for value, not for your time.*

Everyone nods. Nobody argues, because there is nothing left to argue with — the sentence is settled canon, and the only thing you can do with settled canon is comply or look afraid.

It is also right. Billing hours prices the wrong object — it fines you for getting faster, caps you at the number of hours a body has, and invites the buyer to audit your calendar instead of your result. I have never met a serious argument against the destination.

What I want to take away from it is not the destination.

I used to read failed value pricing as a nerve problem. You watch an operator raise the number, hear the pause on the call, and find him back on hourly by Thursday. It looks exactly like fear. So I went hunting for the flinch — wrote a discovery call out in full, to catch the moment the price gets said and the courage leaves.

The flinch was not in the call. I had been looking at the wrong end. What was missing was not nerve at the finish but a number at the start.

Here is the exercise, and you can run it tonight on a call of your own.

One thing first: be exact about what mine is worth. I wrote the call. It is a composite, built out of the moves these conversations make, and a call I wrote cannot return a verdict — I chose every phrase in it, including the ones that end up nowhere. What it can do is show the shape of the test cheaply. The verdict has to come off a call you didn't write.

Forty-five minutes. On one side, a woman who owns a twenty-eight-person engineering firm that has shipped infrastructure work for seventeen years. On the other, a consultant nine months into an AI strategy practice. First time they have spoken.

Sort every phrase that names something. Left: the vocabulary of the market. Right: anything naming the buyer's own operating reality in terms that could be counted. Questions and pleasantries go in neither column.

Left, in order of appearance:

> AI-driven analytics in the new RFPs · intelligent infrastructure analytics platforms · AI-augmented risk modeling · predictive maintenance dashboards · that kind of stack · the technology has gotten enterprise-grade · haven't built out the AI capability · that next wave of RFPs

> codifying it into models · analytics at scale · It's not replacing your engineering judgment. It's amplifying it. · a strategic AI roadmap engagement · map your current capability stack · the highest-leverage AI integration points · a phased roadmap · a stakeholder-aligned implementation plan · we're a strong fit

Right, complete:

> instead of a senior engineer reading every RFP individually, the model can flag the structural risks

One entry, and he said it. It is a real claim about how her firm runs — senior engineers, hours, every RFP — and it arrives as a feature of his product, not a fact about her business. Neither of them asks how many hours, how many RFPs, or what those hours cost.

The first entry on the left is hers. She opens in the vocabulary because it is the language her bid documents arrive in. Two turns later she does ask what one of the phrases means, plainly, on the record. What she never asks is whether it describes her problem at all. Those are different questions, and only the second is expensive.

Now the count — the part that survives the fact that I wrote it, because anyone can run it on a call they didn't write, and what comes back is not a matter of taste. Count every number spoken aloud, and mark which side of the transaction it describes.

Four to six weeks: his assessment. Twelve to eighteen months: his roadmap. About six firms a year: his capacity. Twenty-five thousand: his fee.

Numbers describing her business: zero.

One number is on her side of the desk. A printed RFP lies there, submission deadline seven weeks out. She looks at it right after he names his fee. She does not say it.

Underneath that sits the real one. Her win rate on public-sector bids has fallen roughly forty percent in three years, because the procurement language moved and her firm does not speak it natively. Her problem is how her work gets read, not what her engineers run on their laptops.

That sentence appears nowhere in the forty-five minutes. She never says it. He never reaches for it. The vocabulary was fluent enough on both sides that the problem looked already named.

They both leave energized. Three months and twenty-five thousand dollars later, the win rate has not moved. Nobody misbehaved. He delivered what he sold, on time, forty-two clean pages of it.

Now put a pricing model on that call. Any of them — hourly, fixed fee, outcome-tied with a refund clause. It makes no difference which, because each is a claim about the same missing thing.

**A price is a claim about a number. That call produced no number to make a claim about.**

This is the half the canon leaves out. Value is not a property of your work. It is a movement in a number that belongs to the buyer, and if the number was never said out loud, *charge for value* has nothing to fasten to.

What it collapses into is *charge more.* Which is what most people hear in the room, and what most people go home and do.

Then the engagement lands, nothing moves, and they decide value pricing does not work in their market. It worked exactly as designed. It was bolted onto a conversation with nothing underneath it.

Everything that went wrong later was decided in those forty-five minutes. Not in the fee, not in the contract, not in the scope of work. The price inherited a problem settled before anyone reached for a number.

Her number would have been dull and specific. Win rate, last year against this year. Hours a senior person burns on something that recurs. Not a valuation and not a vision — a quantity she already tracks. And she has to be the one who says it, because a number you supply on her behalf is a guess wearing her clothes.

So the discipline is not pricing courage. It is refusing to leave a call without her number in your notes — and being willing, sometimes, to say there is nothing here to sell yet.

That is a worse quarter and a better decade, and nobody puts it on a slide, because a slide has no room for the part where you leave the money.

Back in the circle, the sentence is still true. It just hands you the last move and calls it the whole game.

The price is the last thing you decide. The number is the first, and it was never yours to say.
