Somebody says it at the end of a long day, usually to somebody who is tired of their job: *marketing is change work.* It lands well. It always does. The room softens — because the sentence was built to soften rooms. It takes a job people are faintly ashamed of and files it under helping.

It is worth knowing the sentence is already a blend. Seth Godin's actual line in *This Is Marketing* is *"marketing is the act of making change happen."* The version that circulates swapped his plain phrase for "change work" — vocabulary borrowed from therapy, hypnotherapy and NLP, where "doing change work with a client" is a term of art.

The whole sentence rests on a word imported from a room marketing has never been in, and the borrowed word is the one doing the work.

Take it seriously for a minute and it stops being a comfort.

*"X is a kind of Y"* is not a compliment. It's a membership claim, and membership claims come with conditions attached. Say your product is a medical device and a regulator appears. Say your business is a bank and a shelf of law arrives. Say your work is change work and you have just claimed a body of practice with a century of findings in it, most of them inconvenient.

The standard reading skips all of that. It hears *change work* and takes the warm half: you're not an interrupter, you're a helper; not extracting, serving. Which is a fine thing to feel and a poor thing to work from, because it makes no demand of you. **A sentence that only makes you feel better isn't a definition. It's a mood.**

## The split

I should say that my first verdict was harsher than the one below, and it did not survive the checking. I wrote that change work has entry conditions — the person asked, the destination is theirs, there's a relationship over time — and that marketing meets none of them, so the compliment was unearned.

Then I tried to point at the pages, and the pages pointed back: court-mandated treatment, family interventions, corporate change programmes, public health campaigns. Change work done on people who didn't ask is a large, named, well-populated branch with its own literature. "The person asked" is not an entry condition at all. Marketing doesn't fail the test. Something more uncomfortable is true.

Change work isn't one thing. It splits, hard, on a single question:

**Did the person ask?**

On the near side: therapy, coaching, the personal trainer, the consultant. Somebody identified a problem, made an appointment, and paid. Every technique on that side assumes an asker. Motivation is a given, not a project. The literature is about what to *do* with someone who wants to change.

The far side is also real, also named, and has its own shelf: court-mandated treatment, family interventions, corporate change programmes, public health campaigns. Nobody asked. Nobody made an appointment. Somebody else decided this person should be different, and the work begins on a person who never signed up.

That branch is harder in one specific way, and it's the way that matters: **its first problem is one the near side never has** — getting the person to take part at all. Its literature is disproportionately about engagement, resistance and dropout, because that's where it loses.

The corporate version supplies the statistic everybody quotes about change programmes failing; treat the number as folklore, because it is, but nobody working in the field argues that involuntary change is the easy kind.

The far side is where change work goes to have a bad time. That's the side marketing starts on.

## The funnel is the crossing

Somebody scrolling past your ad did not ask. Somebody who gets your email because they once bought a lamp did not ask. Somebody who has never heard of the problem you solve isn't merely uninformed — they're standing on the far side of that line, and every finding about that side applies to them.

But somebody typing *"best CRM for a five-person team"* **asked.** They crossed. Somebody reading three comparison pages at eleven at night has walked in and sat down.

That broke my repaired verdict too. I had rewritten the claim as change work *done on people who never asked* — and "never asked" is false for a large, valuable and entirely ordinary chunk of marketing. The line had to move again.

So the line doesn't run between marketing and change work. **It runs through the middle of marketing** — and the entire top half of the discipline turns out to be machinery for carrying one person across it.

Permission marketing is a crossing machine. So is the lead magnet, and SEO, and the free tool, and the newsletter somebody actually opens, and the article that names a problem the reader hadn't named yet. Every one of them is doing the same job: converting a non-asker into an asker, so that the near side's methods become available at all.

That's not a small reframe. It says the top of your funnel has exactly one job, and the job is not persuasion.

Here's the part that costs something.

The far side has a standard method, worked out over decades on precisely the population marketing works on: people who didn't ask and don't currently want to. It's called motivational interviewing, and its central finding is the one every marketer will hate.

Miller and Rollnick named the primary error the **righting reflex** — the trained, well-meant, nearly irresistible urge to supply the argument. To explain why they should change. To marshal the reasons, handle the objection, make the case.

The finding, repeated across forty years of trials, is that the righting reflex *reduces* the odds of change. Argue for it and the person argues back — and people believe what they hear themselves say. You don't merely fail to persuade. You recruit them into the opposite position, in their own voice, which is the most durable form there is.

Now look at a marketing department. Making the case *is the entire output.* Benefits, reasons to believe, objections handled, the comparison table, the rebuttal to the competitor. **Marketing is the righting reflex, industrialised, at scale, with a budget.**

The far side's actual method is to stop arguing and start eliciting: ask, reflect back, let the person state the gap between what they want and what they're doing, and get out of the way the moment they start arguing for the change themselves.

Marketing has a small, unfashionable tradition that does exactly this — the quiz that shows you your own number, the diagnostic, the calculator, the audit, the question used as a headline. Almost nobody builds it, because it doesn't look like work and it can't be compressed into a slogan.

## The tell

One last thing, and it's how you can tell the sentence hasn't actually been accepted by anybody yet.

If you genuinely believed the top of your funnel was a crossing machine, you would measure crossings. You'd keep a number for *people who did not have this problem in their head on Monday and did by Friday.* You'd defend that number in a meeting.

Nobody has it. The stack measures impressions — activity on the far side, no crossing implied — and conversions, which happen well after the crossing already occurred. **The one event the whole model turns on is the only event with no metric anywhere in it.**

Which is exactly what you'd expect if the sentence were still being used as a compliment. Run the near side's playbook at people standing on the far side; and when the change doesn't take, install a structure instead of doing the work — auto-renew, the default-on checkbox, the cancellation flow with four screens. Those aren't change work. They're what you put in when the change never happened and you'd rather not find out.

Marketing is change work. It's just the hard kind, and nobody asked.
