If you make things with words for a living, you have watched the price of making them fall through the floor. Copy, ads, landing pages, whole content calendars — what took a team a month now takes a model a minute.

And the standard reading of this, on the panic side and the hype side alike, is the same reading: when the thing you sell becomes free to produce, the money in it evaporates.

There is a 1980 economics paper that says the standard reading is wrong — not too gloomy, not too rosy, wrong — and says precisely where the money goes instead.

## What the paper actually proved

Sanford Grossman and Joseph Stiglitz published it under a title that is also its thesis: *On the Impossibility of Informationally Efficient Markets.* The argument is almost too clean.

Imagine a market where some traders pay a cost to learn something true about an asset, and everyone else just watches the price. The watchers free-ride: when the informed traders act, they move the price, and the price quietly tells everyone else what they paid to find out.

So the more people who pay to be informed, the more the secret leaks into the price, and the smaller the reward for having paid. The reward shrinks until, at the margin, it exactly covers the cost of getting informed — and not a penny more.

Now push it to the limit. If the price reflected everything, the reward for getting informed would be zero — so no one would bother — so the price couldn't reflect everything. A perfectly efficient market is self-erasing. It can't exist, because existing would destroy the only thing that builds it.

Here is the paper's own sentence, the one that carries the whole result:

> "Because information is costly, prices cannot perfectly reflect the information which is available, since if it did, those who spent resources to obtain it would receive no compensation. There is a fundamental conflict between the efficiency with which markets spread information and the incentives to acquire information."

What you get instead is a market held in permanent, deliberate imperfection — their phrase for it is "an equilibrium degree of disequilibrium" — efficient enough to be useful, never so efficient that finding things out stops paying.

The reward for knowing is pinned, by the mechanism itself, to what the knowing cost. Economists call that persistent payment a rent: money that keeps flowing to whoever bears a cost nobody else will bear.

Now map it onto 2026. The cost of getting informed is the cost of finding an angle that works — the testing budget, the dead creative, the hours. The price everyone watches is your running winner: the ad still live in the library, the funnel that's obviously converting.

Scaling it is the only way to cash it, and scaling it is exactly the act that broadcasts it. There's no quiet way to win. The louder the win, the louder the leak.

Which turns "we have AI" inside out. A low cost isn't your protection — it's the murder weapon.

The reward you can earn is the size of the cost others would have to pay to catch up, and "we have AI" doesn't mean you found a private edge. It means the cost of finding edges just collapsed, and it collapsed for everyone in the same breath.

You're not announcing a secret. You're announcing that secrets in your category are now cheap.

## Where the received reading breaks

I went back to the paper before trusting that story, and the popular version of it breaks in two places worth naming.

First, the attribution. I've seen sharp write-ups of this exact problem credit the whole collapse to "Stiglitz, exactly" — including the part where your buyer now runs your pitch through their own AI before committing. That part is real and important, but it's a different theorem.

Rivals copying your visible winner is Grossman–Stiglitz, genuinely. A buyer who couldn't check quality before, suddenly handed a tool that checks it for them, is Akerlof's market for lemons.

The mislabel matters because the two collapses demand opposite responses. Against competitors, your friend is illegibility — make it hard to read how you did it. Against the buyer's inspection, illegibility is suicide: you can't beat the screen, so the only winning move is to pass it on purpose — say the quiet part out loud, name the risk, hand over the proof their AI was about to demand anyway.

Hide how you found it; show that it works.

Second, the analogy has an honest crack. Real markets have a single price that provably carries the information. Marketing doesn't.

A competitor watching your live ad sees a blurry, lagging shadow — the headline, not your true economics, not even which of your ten live ads is the actual winner. So "the content market is now efficient" is a metaphor, not a mechanism.

What survives when you drop the metaphor is the plainer finding finance has measured directly: published edges decay, fast, and survive only where they're expensive to copy. That part transfers cleanly. The elegant math doesn't.

## The conservation law

The deepest thing in the paper isn't the impossibility. It's a conservation law hiding inside it: the reward doesn't disappear when a cost collapses — it migrates to whatever stays expensive.

When generating the thing goes to roughly free, the money relocates to the one cost a machine can't drive to zero: knowing whether what got generated is any good. Telling the real winner from the confident fake. That discernment is now the scarce thing, and scarcity is where rent lives.

Three consequences fall out, and they're concrete. The flood is the demand, not the threat: the most counter-intuitive result in the paper is that more noise in a market — more randomness muddying what the price reveals — pulls in more informed players, not fewer.

Translated: the more the channel fills with fluent output that reads beautifully and quietly fails, the more people will pay for someone who can tell the difference. Rising AI fluency isn't eating the judgment business. It's sizing it.

But judgment leaks too. A published verdict is itself a kind of price — show enough of them and someone reverse-engineers the method and stops paying. The defensible version of selling discernment is the version that doesn't fully reveal itself when delivered: the part that compounds quietly, updates faster than it leaks, and traces back to a body of work nobody can cheaply rebuild.

And a permanent edge is forbidden outright — that's the impossible corner of the theorem. Any "here's the secret, one-time payment" product is selling a thing guaranteed to decay, and guaranteed to enrage its buyers when it does. The only structurally honest offer is continuous re-acquisition of a thin, perishable edge.

Don't sell the edge. Sell the engine that keeps finding the next one.

So carry one test. A claimed moat survives only if it violates one of the conditions that make edges decay — a signal that doesn't leak when you use it, a barrier that isn't about information at all (brand, distribution, relationships, scale), or a cost the imitator simply can't pay. Pick any AI-era offer, including your own, and ask which of those it actually has.

If the answer is none, it's a phantom. It feels like a wall, and you're already standing on the wrong side of it.
